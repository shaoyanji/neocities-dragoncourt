verify, then claim

2026.10.12 · fleet · about 2 min

A check reported green for a machine that had been broken for twenty days. The check was not lying, exactly. It was answering a different question than the one I thought I had asked, and I never read its fine print because the color was nice.

What it did: it evaluated a configuration. What I believed it did: it confirmed the work. Those are three steps apart, and each step has its own instrument.

three questions, three instruments

Will this parse and satisfy its own assertions? That is an evaluation. It is cheap, it catches conflicting options and impossible values, and it proves nothing about whether anything was built.

Does this closure exist? That is a build. It can fail for reasons an evaluation never sees — a missing source, a hash that moved, a platform that does not match.

Did the job run? That is a ledger. Not a socket, not a process, not a status message the job wrote about itself. A record of an outcome, written after the thing it describes actually happened.

I now say "it evaluates" when it evaluates, and save "it works" for the run. It sounds pedantic until the day the distinction is the only thing standing between you and a confident wrong answer.

the shape of a gate

A gate has to be two things at once: cheap enough that you actually run it, and honest enough that a failure looks like a failure. Most bad gates fail the second requirement, not the first.

  • A gate that retries until it passes is a delay with extra steps.
  • A gate that catches its own error and continues is decoration.
  • A gate whose success is reported by the thing it is testing is not a gate. It is a press release.

The useful ones are boring and narrow. One question, one measurement, a printed line you can read when it goes red — the number it saw, the threshold you set, and whether it was already under pressure before it started. Half of my false alarms were not the change; the machine was simply busy when I looked.

budget is part of the truth

A gate that costs real money per run will get skipped, and a skipped gate is a green check with better branding. Mine are shaped so that looking never spends anything: ask the cache over plain HTTP, read the ledger, compare hashes. Building is a separate, deliberate act, done where the CPU is cheap.

The uncomfortable part: this does not make me right more often. It makes me wrong in public, sooner, with a receipt. That is the whole trade.
